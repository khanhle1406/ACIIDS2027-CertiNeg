Certified Absence: Design-Based Risk Control
         for Semantic NOT EXISTS Queries

      Abstract. A grouped semantic NOT EXISTS query can return a false
       claim of absence after missing just one positive record. We formalize certi-
      fied absence as finite-population control of the group-level false-discovery
       proportion (FDP) of a frozen output set. CertiNeg samples candidate
      groups without replacement, resolves sampled group truth exactly, and
       uses hypergeometric inversion with simultaneous or fixed-sequence mul-
        tiple testing over predeclared nested outputs. Proxy scores determine
       inspection order; the guarantee does not require them to be calibrated.
      Exhaustive enumeration verifies coverage; a boundary case with true
    FDP 0.10083 has exact false-certification probability 0.049745 at (ε, δ) =
       (0.10, 0.05). On a controlled population, fixed-sequence certification re-
       tains 79.2% versus 48.6% for independent sample splitting. On SemBench
       Cars, a complete screen of 15 frequent complaint predicates exposes both
       certifiable and abstention regimes; four comparable nondegenerate work-
       loads have 1,093–1,643 candidate groups and unsafe full-output FDPs
        of 14.6–16.9%. Across five group-disjoint AIR BAGS splits, mean cer-
       tified retention is 64.1% ± 3.9% with no unsafe return in 10,000 audit
       randomizations. Utility varies with grouping and risk targets. A frozen
      0.6B-parameter LLM pilot produces no candidate absences, so certifica-
       tion cannot recover useful output from that executor.

1  Introduction

Query engines now use foundation models to evaluate predicates over structured
and unstructured data. LOTUS formalizes these semantic operators [9], and Sem-
Bench benchmarks engines that implement them [7]. Grouped negative queries
arise naturally in this setting: a user might ask for camera-days with no image
of a target species, cars with no indication of a safety issue in any modality, or
customer accounts with no document satisfying a semantic condition.
   These queries are vulnerable to missed positives. In a group with only one
positive record, a single row-level false negative can cause the engine to report
absence for the whole group. High record-level accuracy or recall therefore gives
no direct answer to the user’s question: what fraction of the returned groups
actually contain a positive? We measure error at the returned-group level.

   Evergreen already supports grouped semantic claim verification, witness
stopping, finite-population sampling, confidence sequences, and multiplicity con-
trol [8]; risk-limiting audits provide sequential and weighted finite-population
tools [13,12]. We use these established mechanisms to study the FDP of a frozen
returned set of negative groups.
   Let A be the committed output of a semantic NOT EXISTS query. We ask
whether the fraction of returned groups that secretly contain a positive record is
at most ε, with audit risk δ. The guarantee concerns false discoveries across the
returned set; per-claim scores and model posteriors answer different questions.
   Our contributions are:

 1. We formalize certified semantic absence as a finite-population design-based
    guarantee on the group-level FDP of a frozen negative-query output.
 2. We use probability-sampled outer groups and exact inner verification; proxies
   may reduce inspection cost but never enter validity.
 3. We combine exact hypergeometric  testing with simultaneous  or fixed-
    sequence selection over predeclared nested outputs, and characterize exact-
    negative cost including the near-census burden at ε = 0.
 4. We provide exhaustive tests and controlled/SemBench experiments covering
    tightness, baselines, audit budget, family granularity, target sensitivity, pred-
    icate screening, split/ranking robustness, alternative grouping, and a frozen
   small-LLM pilot.


2  Background and Related Work

2.1  Semantic query processing

LOTUS provides declarative semantic operators [9], and SemBench benchmarks
semantic query engines [7]. CertiNeg operates after such an execution layer
freezes a negative candidate set.
  SUPG certifies proxy-assisted precision/recall [6], BARGAIN provides guar-
antees for LLM cascades [14], and Semantic Views certifies semantic-filter preci-
sion/recall from fresh oracle samples [4]. For nonempty outputs, our FDP equals
one minus precision when each group is treated as a unit with label 1 −Zg.
Thus grouped-absence certification can use precision-control tools at the group
level. Obtaining exact group labels is the operational difficulty. One positive wit-
ness refutes absence, but confirming absence requires checking every record. Our
study concerns the resulting selection and cost trade-off; the generic precision
guarantee is already available. Run-to-run query variation motivates freezing the
candidate output before audit [3].


2.2  Claim verification versus output-set certification

Evergreen is the closest systems work [8]: it verifies cardinal, proportional, and
grouped semantic claims using witness stopping, finite-population sampling, con-
fidence sequences, and multiplicity control.

  We estimate FDP across the returned negative set and resolve each audited
group’s truth exactly. The semantic proxy affects inspection cost while the outer
confidence bound uses only audited group labels. Selection is restricted to a
predeclared nested family. Confidence sequences, multiplicity correction, and
witness-first ordering are established tools.


2.3  Finite-population and prediction-assisted inference

Sampling without replacement provides the finite-population basis  [2]; risk-
limiting audits add sequential and weighted tools [13,12]. Learn-then-Test devel-
ops fixed-sequence calibration [1]; conformal selection controls false selections
under exchangeability [5]. Our guarantee conditions on one fixed corpus. Its only
randomness comes from the audit design, so it bounds output-set FDP for that
corpus without a model-distribution guarantee.

4  CertiNeg

4.1  Freeze first, audit second

Before any audit label is requested, the implementation commits the data snap-
shot, query, semantic plan, model identifiers, prompts/decoding settings, candi-
date groups, risk scores, and code version. A production artifact can record these
commitments as hashes in a candidate_groups.jsonl manifest. The query and
threshold must then remain fixed when audit evidence arrives. Documented run-
to-run variation in semantic queries makes this commitment necessary [3].

4.5  Ordered fixed-sequence selection

If the application accepts only the largest consecutively certified prefix, standard
fixed-sequence testing [1] avoids the δ/K penalty. Test A1, . . . , AK at level δ in
the predeclared order, stop at the first Uk > ε, and return the last passing set.

Theorem 3 (Ordered fixed-sequence guarantee). The fixed-sequence rule
above satisfies Eq. (4).

Proof. If every Ak is safe, the violation event is empty. Otherwise let j be the
first index with FDP(Aj) > ε. Returning any unsafe set requires the procedure
to reach j and falsely certify Aj. The latter event is contained in the level-δ
false-certification event of the fixed-set test for Aj, whose probability is at most
δ. Dependence among tests does not enlarge this event.                             ⊓⊔

We use fixed-sequence testing by default when consecutive certification is accept-
able. Simultaneous protection allows the auditor to skip a failed intermediate
threshold. Sequential e-process and weighted audit constructions can improve
other operating points [13,12].

5  Prototype and Reproducibility

The prototype is implemented in Python with NumPy and SciPy. The core confi-
dence routine inverts the hypergeometric CDF by monotone binary search. One
sampled group set from AK can feed equal- or weighted-Bonferroni simultane-
ous bounds, or the ordered fixed-sequence rule. No model probability enters the
confidence calculation.
   Our reproducibility package contains code, exhaustive validity tests, frozen
experiment inputs, machine-readable results, and figure scripts with fixed seeds.
A production interface should additionally persist an immutable candidate man-
ifest and replayable audit trace.


6  Experimental Methodology

6.1  Research questions

We examine exact coverage (RQ1), the mismatch between row and group errors
(RQ2), selection utility against valid baselines (RQ3), audit cost and family
granularity (RQ4), and sensitivity to predicates, splits, ranking and risk targets
(RQ5).


6.2  Controlled populations

For RQ1, we exhaustively enumerate all defect counts and all possible sample
outcomes for every N ≤30, every 0 ≤n ≤N, and α ∈{0.10, 0.05, 0.01}. We
also save a larger sparse grid through N = 40.
   For RQ2, we generate 1,500 groups with heavy-tailed sizes, mark 62% as
containing at least one positive, and simulate a semantic row predicate with
target positive-row recall 0.92 and false-positive probability 0.002. A group enters
the predicted-absence output only if no row is predicted positive. This simulation
illustrates the error mechanism; it does not measure a particular LLM.

7  Results

7.1  Exact coverage and zero-FDP burden

The exhaustive unit test found no undercoverage. Across every N ≤30 and
every sample size, the minimum coverages were 0.9000, 0.9500, and 0.9900 for
nominal levels 0.90, 0.95, and 0.99 respectively. A boundary calculation also
shows the fixed-set test need not be very conservative: at N = 1200, n = 800,
M = 121 (true FDP 0.10083), ε = 0.10, and δ = 0.05, exact hypergeometric
summation gives false-certification probability 0.049745. The test can therefore
operate close to its nominal risk even though the frozen experiments below record
zero violations.
   For zero tolerated FDP, the exact calculation requires near-census sam-
pling: for N = 1000, observing zero defects requires n = 900, 950, 990 at
δ = 0.10, 0.05, 0.01, respectively; the artifact tabulates the same integer-rounded
result for N ∈{50, 100, 500, 1000}. A semantic confidence score alone cannot
reduce this sampling requirement.


7.2  Row accuracy does not answer the group question

The controlled row-predicate experiment attained positive-row recall 0.9176. It
returned 586 candidate-absence groups, of which 0.1024 contained at least one
positive record. This simulated result shows how high row recall can coexist
with substantial error in the returned set. It does not estimate group FDP for a
production semantic engine. With existential semantics, the number and depen-
dence of positives within each group matter.


7.3  Controlled selected-set certification

The ordered fixed-sequence policy retains 0.792 on average (median 0.80) on
the frozen population. The full candidate set has true FDP 0.1158 and violates
ε = 0.10, whereas the 80% prefix has FDP 0.0781. No unsafe set was returned
in 2,000 repetitions; the one-sided 95% Clopper–Pearson upper bound on the
empirical violation frequency is 0.00150. These repetitions test the procedure
on the frozen population. Equation (4) remains the guarantee; the boundary
calculation above checks near-nominal type-I behavior of the fixed-set test.
   The 80% set was selected in 96.0% of audits and the 60% set in 4.0%; no
audit returned the unsafe 100% set. The fixed-sequence rule can therefore exploit
the predeclared order without dividing δ by K.

7.6  Proxy and risk-ranking sensitivity

The witness-first proxy reduces record inspections but cannot remove the cost
of exhausting true negatives. At the main controlled operating point, proxy or-
dering saves 4.73% relative to random inner order while the audit still consumes
66.6% of exact-census labels; on AIR BAGS the corresponding values are 7.45%
and 35.0%. The census ratios follow from SRSWOR in expectation (Eq. (14));
the 65% reduction on AIR BAGS comes from auditing a subset of groups, inde-
pendently of the proxy’s inspection-order savings.
   Group-risk scores determine which prefixes are useful; validity holds for any
frozen ranking. On AIR BAGS at a 35% audit, the original semantic ordering
retains 0.630 on average. Adding Gaussian score noise with standard deviation
equal to one or three empirical score standard deviations reduces retention to
0.347 ± 0.087 and 0.017 ± 0.028 across ten frozen noisy rankings; fully random

8.2  Frozen small-LLM pilot

We also evaluate Qwen3-0.6B [10] at a pinned model revision on 16 calibration
and 64 held-out parent groups selected by SHA-256 ordering independently of
labels. All 9,018 complaints in those parents are retained. The prompt contains
complaint text and either AIR BAGS or EXTERIOR LIGHTING, never the
reference label. With thinking disabled, the normalized Yes/No next-token like-
lihood supplies the score; inputs are capped at 512 tokens (1.75% truncated).
Thresholds are the eighth percentile of positive calibration scores, fixed before
evaluating the held-out corpus. The two grouping keys and two audit budgets
are unchanged.
   The pilot produces no candidate negative groups for either predicate or group-
ing. Row F1 is .083 and .048. All eight planned LLM configurations therefore
have empty output; their zero unsafe-return counts provide no evidence of useful
certification. On the same held-out subset, the matched TF–IDF/SGD baseline
retains .052/.599 for AIR BAGS and .208/.670 for EXTERIOR LIGHTING us-
ing fuel-type groups at 35%/two-thirds audit. The small executor and calibration
rule therefore fail before the audit stage. We preserve the complete cache and
report every configuration. We do not tune on test labels, and empty output
counts as no useful certification. The GPU run takes 1,022 seconds including
model loading on one T4; audit sweeps run on CPU.


9  Discussion and Limitations

Scope and cost. The guarantee applies to the recorded corpus and its reference
labels. When ε is near zero, rankings are weak, or clean groups are large, a census
may be preferable to a sampled audit. Confirming a negative group still requires
checking every record, and outputs must remain frozen once audit labels are
exposed.

Evaluation limits. We evaluate one benchmark, using attribute-based groups
and benchmark fields as reference labels. TF–IDF/SGD produces useful certified
outputs; the small-LLM pilot produces none. Evaluation with larger LLMs and
VLMs, other datasets, and human oracles is still needed, including measurements
of oracle disagreement and latency. Sequential or weighted audit designs also
warrant study.

Novelty boundary. Record-filter guarantees [6,14,4], fixed-sequence testing [1],
and Evergreen’s quantified claim verification [8] are prior art. Evergreen-style per-
group verification asks whether individual claims are correct; our objective allows
controlled nonzero FDP over a frozen returned set and chooses how much output
to retain. A direct systems comparison would require a shared query/compiler
objective. Our contribution is to specify the finite-population target for grouped
absence, connect it to exact group verification, and measure how validity, re-
tained output and audit cost interact.

10  Conclusion

CertiNeg certifies the group-level FDP of a frozen semantic absence output
using exact group verification and standard finite-population tests. Exhaustive
tests verify coverage, and a boundary calculation places false-certification proba-
bility close to the nominal risk. Experiments measure the audit budget needed to
retain output and show how selection, ranking and grouping affect utility. These
choices do not enter the validity calculation. The LLM pilot produces no candi-
date absences, leaving nothing useful to certify. The results call for evaluation
with more models and datasets.
